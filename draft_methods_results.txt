METHODS AND RESULTS
Draft: avian plumage colour and land-use change
================================================================================
Four analyses (community colour, colour-abundance, colour-abundance change, and a phylogenetic test). Each is run twice: once with Cooney UVS colour (the A analyses, primary) and once with the independent Dale plumage scores (the B analyses, a colour-source robustness check), reported in separate sections. Model codes in parentheses map to scripts and ANALYSIS_NOTES.md.

STATUS, 2026-08-14. The refit is complete. All 35 models across both colour families converged with zero divergent transitions and a maximum Rhat of 1.012, and all results and figures below come from the corrected dataset. One diagnostic problem is outstanding and is described under Analysis 1: every species-level model, in both families, returned a low energy Bayesian fraction of missing information.

METHODS
================================================================================

Data

We combined the PREDICTS database (site-level bird survey records from studies spanning multiple land-use types worldwide) with Cooney et al.'s spectrophotometric plumage measurements (ultraviolet-sensitive [UVS] visual model, LociUVS scores) and AVONET traits (body mass, trophic niche). The merged dataset comprised 216,834 records from 1,703 passerine species. Our primary (A) analyses used the Cooney UVS model. We derived four species-level colour variables: mean colourfulness (mean of male and female LociUVS), male colourfulness (male LociUVS), the dichromatism ratio (male over female LociUVS), and sexual dichromatism (male minus female LociUVS, which can be negative). We then repeated every analysis with the independent Dale et al. (2015) plumage scores in place of the UVS metrics, as a colour-source robustness check reported separately (the B analyses, see Dale-colour replication).

We collapsed the WWF biome field into three categories (Biome4): Tropical closed (moist and dry broadleaf and coniferous forests, the reference), Tropical open (tropical grasslands, savannas, shrublands, and mangroves), and Temperate closed (temperate broadleaf, mixed and conifer forests, and Mediterranean forests, woodlands and scrub). A fourth category, Temperate open (temperate and montane grasslands, tundra), was excluded. It contributed 115 primary-vegetation records against 36, 42, 0 and 35 records in the four modified land uses, so its biome by land-use interaction was not estimable and no collapse of those cells left a defensible contrast. Excluding it cost 0.9% of records. Land use (five levels) was Primary vegetation (reference), Secondary, Plantation, Cropland, and Pasture. Body mass was log-transformed and standardised (z_logMass). Trophic niche (AVONET) entered as a fixed factor (Aquatic predator reference).

Site-level analyses used records with non-zero abundance, in the retained biomes, from publications containing primary vegetation plus at least one other land use (n = 25,864 records, 1,420 species, "present.csv"). The biome exclusion preceded the primary-vegetation filter, so each publication was judged on the records that enter the models. Land-use representation was Primary vegetation 10,805, Plantation 6,482, Secondary 5,733, Cropland 2,225, and Pasture 619. All fifteen biome by land-use cells were estimable, the thinnest being Tropical open by Pasture at 96 records from 34 species. Pasture is nonetheless sparse, because one contributing publication holding 1,387 pasture records contained no primary vegetation and was therefore excluded, and we interpret pasture effects with corresponding caution.

All models used the canonical PREDICTS nested random structure: study, block within study, and site within block (SS, SSB, SSBS, 40 studies / 203 blocks / 2,335 sites), which is fully nested because these identifiers are hierarchically coded. Analyses 2 to 4 additionally included a phylogenetic random effect from the BigBirdTree tree (BBtree2, 9,072 tips, Jetz taxonomy) as a Brownian-motion covariance matrix A (ape::vcv.phylo, correlation form). Species were matched to tips in three stages (jetz_sp, then Best_guess_binomial, then a curated genus-synonym table), with no grafting, and tips claimed by more than one species were dropped. Matching retained 1,157 species for Analysis 2a and 1,154 for Analysis 2b. Records whose colour metric was missing were then dropped by the sampler, so the fitted models carry 1,043 species (19,785 records) for Analysis 2a and 1,041 species (50,534 records) for Analysis 2b. At the species level, where the association scores are built from detections, the tree retained 1,375 species, of which 1,242 have Cooney colour data and 1,372 have Dale scores.

Statistical framework

Models were fitted in brms/CmdStan. Colourfulness and dichromatism-ratio responses, and relative abundance, used a lognormal family. Sexual dichromatism and the paired abundance difference used a Gaussian family. Effects are termed credible when the 95% posterior credible interval (CI) excludes zero. Bayesian R2 is reported as posterior median [95% CI].

Post-hoc contrasts and effect sizes

Model coefficients answer only the questions their contrast coding happens to encode, and in a model with interactions those are conditional on the reference levels. We therefore report differences between factor levels as posterior contrasts, computed as follows and implemented in scripts/cooney/posthoc_helpers.R.

For each factor we build an estimated marginal mean per level by evaluating the linear predictor over a balanced reference grid of the remaining factors, with random effects excluded (re_formula = NA), body mass held at its mean, and the standardised colour predictors of Analyses 2 and 3 held at zero. Averaging is balanced rather than weighted by cell frequency, so the contrasts describe the fitted model rather than the sampling design. We then difference the posterior draws and summarise the resulting distribution. Because these are posterior probabilities of a difference rather than repeated frequentist tests, no family-wise correction is applied and none is required, so the Tukey and Bonferroni adjustments have no role here.

Table 1 contrasts every pair of levels of one factor at a time, averaged over the others. Table 2 holds one factor fixed and contrasts the levels of the other within it, giving every land-use pair within each biome and every biome pair within each land use. Table 2 also reports the colour slope per standard deviation within each biome and each land use for Analyses 2 and 3, obtained as the linear predictor at +0.5 and -0.5 standard deviations differenced, and interaction contrasts, which are differences between two such simple effects and test whether a land-use conversion costs the same in two biomes. Contrasts in which biome and land use both differ were not computed, because they confound the two factors and a credible result among them could not be attributed to either.

Alongside every contrast we report the posterior probability of direction, the proportion of draws sharing the sign of the estimate, and a standardised effect size defined as the estimate divided by the standard deviation of that response on the modelled scale. That denominator is the total standard deviation including study and site variation, so these effect sizes are conservative relative to conventions that divide by the residual alone. The column definitions and their interpretation are given in full in Tables_column_guide.txt.

Figures were produced by scripts/cooney/pub_figures.R from the fitted objects and these contrast tables. The forest panels of Figures 6 and 5 plot contrasts rather than model coefficients, grouped into labelled blocks. Where a block shares one reference level the strip names it, so the rows of the three biome blocks in Figure 6 are each land use against primary vegetation within that biome and the guild rows are each guild against aquatic predators. Where a row names both sides it does so explicitly with "vs". Two blocks hold slopes rather than contrasts, body mass in Figure 6 and the colour slopes in Figure 5, and their strips say so. In Figure 3 the fitted lines are model predictions drawn from the phylogenetic posterior rather than least squares through the species points, exploiting the fact that the compositional model is linear in the association scores. They are drawn solid across the range of scores observed and dotted beyond it. Species scoring zero for a land use are omitted from that panel, since a zero carries no information about the level of the response at nonzero scores and would otherwise mass at the axis.

Analysis 1: Phylogenetic association of colour with land use (A1b)

At the species level, we regressed each colour response on the within-species proportion of records in each land use (five compositional proportions summing to 1), using a no-intercept parameterisation so each coefficient is the expected colour for a species recorded exclusively in that land use:

    colour ~ 0 + prop_Cropland + prop_Pasture + prop_Plantation_forest
                + prop_Primary_vegetation + prop_Secondary + (1 | gr(phylo, cov = A))

These proportions were computed across the full raw dataset rather than the filtered analytical dataset, because a species' habitat association is a property of the species and is best estimated from all of its records. Within that dataset we counted only records where the species was detected. This matters more than it may appear. PREDICTS stores a near-complete site by species matrix, so 84% of raw rows are structural zeros for species not recorded at a site, and counting every row would measure how a study distributed its sites across land uses rather than where the bird was found. In one study, for example, all 20 species share the same row composition of 116 primary-vegetation and 27 secondary-vegetation rows while their detections range from 13 and 2 to 53 and 9, and 53% of species occur in a single study, so a row-based score would be a constant for them. Priors were broadened for species-level data: Normal(0,5) on fixed effects for the lognormal responses, Normal(0,50) for the Gaussian response, Exponential(2) on the standard deviations and sigma for the lognormal responses, and Exponential(0.05) for the Gaussian response. We ran 4 chains for 4,000 iterations (2,000 warmup) at adapt_delta = 0.95, max_treedepth = 12, for n = 1,242 species. All four models converged on the usual diagnostics, with no divergent transitions, a maximum Rhat of 1.003, and a minimum bulk ESS of 788. All four nonetheless returned an energy Bayesian fraction of missing information (E-BFMI) below 0.3, on all four chains for mean colourfulness, male colourfulness and sexual dichromatism, and on two chains for the dichromatism ratio. A low E-BFMI indicates that the sampler explored the energy distribution poorly, which is common in hierarchical models with a heavy-tailed posterior and is not detected by Rhat or effective sample size. The same warning arose in the Dale replication, so the problem is a property of the species-level phylogenetic regression itself and not of either colour metric. We therefore treat the A1b and B1b posteriors as adequate for central estimates but unreliable in the tails, and we return to this when interpreting the land-use contrasts. Because the compositional coefficients are strongly correlated in the posterior, we compare land uses by contrasting posterior draws rather than by inspecting whether marginal credible intervals overlap, and we report those contrasts with the posterior probability that the difference is positive.

Analysis 2a: Colour as a predictor of relative abundance (A2c)

We tested whether colour predicts a species' share of its community, and whether that share depends on biome or land use. Relative abundance was each species' effort-corrected count divided by its site (SSBS) total, and each colour metric was standardised. For male colourfulness and sexual dichromatism we fitted (lognormal)

    relative_abundance ~ z_colour * Biome4 + z_colour * Predominant_simple
                         + Biome4 * Predominant_simple
                         + (1|SS) + (1|SSB) + (1|SSBS) + (1 | gr(phylo, cov = A))

that is, all three two-way interactions but no three-way term. Priors were as in Analysis 3 (lognormal), with 4 chains by 2,000 iterations (1,000 warmup) at adapt_delta = 0.90. Because colour, trophic niche and body mass are themselves phylogenetically conserved, the colour main effect is interpreted as an effect beyond phylogeny. As a sensitivity check we also fitted the full three-way interaction (model A2e) and compared it to the two-way model by LOO. Both models converged with no divergent transitions, a maximum Rhat of 1.006, and a minimum bulk ESS of 846.

Analysis 2b: Colour and abundance change following land-use conversion (A2d)

To include absences and anchor each species to its own baseline, we computed, per study, each species' mean relative abundance across primary-vegetation sites, and subtracted it from that species' relative abundance at each modified site (the paired difference). Exact-zero differences, meaning species absent from both, were dropped as uninformative. This yielded 66,225 non-zero differences, of which 54,038 were negative. Primary vegetation is thus absorbed into the response, leaving four land-use levels. For the same two colour metrics we fitted the same two-way structure as Analysis 2a with a Gaussian family (n = 50,534). Priors were Normal(0,0.5), Normal(0,0.2), and Exponential(5), and we ran 4 chains for 3,500 iterations (1,500 warmup) at adapt_delta = 0.95, max_treedepth = 12. The three-way interaction was again fitted as a sensitivity check (model A2f) and compared by LOO. Both models converged with no divergent transitions, a maximum Rhat of 1.007, and a minimum bulk ESS of 587. The elevated intercept Rhat seen in the previous fit did not recur.

Analysis 3: Community plumage colour across biomes and land uses (A3b, A3a)

For each of the four colour responses (community-weighted over occurrence records, n = 24,614 with non-missing colour), we fitted two nested models that share the same data, priors, sampler, and random structure. The primary model (A3b) was

    colour ~ Biome4 * Predominant_simple + Trophic.Niche + z_logMass
             + (1 | SS) + (1 | SSB) + (1 | SSBS)

We ran A3b in two variants, a base model with biome by land use only and a covariate model that adds trophic niche and body mass as main effects, and we report the covariate model. The extension (A3a) adds two interaction families to the A3b covariate model, land use by trophic niche and land use by body mass:

    colour ~ Biome4 * Predominant_simple + Trophic.Niche + z_logMass
             + Predominant_simple:z_logMass + Predominant_simple:Trophic.Niche
             + (1 | SS) + (1 | SSB) + (1 | SSBS)

A3a is therefore a nested superset of A3b, and we compared the two by leave-one-out cross-validation (LOO) to test whether the guild-specific and size-specific interactions earn their place. Priors were Normal(0,2) on the intercept, Normal(0,0.5) on fixed effects, and Exponential(2) on the standard deviations and sigma for the lognormal responses, and Normal(0,50), Normal(0,20), Exponential(2) for the Gaussian response. We ran 4 chains for 3,000 iterations (1,500 warmup) at adapt_delta = 0.90. No phylogenetic term was included. Because colour is a species-level constant with no within-species variance, a per-species phylogenetic intercept is not identifiable at the record level, where it absorbs all between-species variance and collapses the residual and site terms. The phylogenetic question is addressed at the species level in Analysis 1. All twelve models converged with no divergent transitions. For A3b the maximum Rhat was 1.008 and the minimum bulk effective sample size (ESS) was 651. For A3a the maximum Rhat was 1.011 and the minimum bulk ESS was 539.

Dale-colour replication (B analyses)

To test whether the findings depend on the colour-scoring system, we repeated all four analyses with Dale et al.'s plumage scores in place of the Cooney UVS metrics, holding the data, priors, sampler, random structure, and metric families identical. These are the B analyses: B3b and B3a replicate A3b and A3a, B2c replicates A2c, B2d replicates A2d, and B1 replicates A1. From each colour system we derived the same four responses (mean colourfulness, male colourfulness, the dichromatism ratio, and sexual dichromatism), with the same lognormal and Gaussian families. Dale scores cover more species than the UVS metrics (1,679 against 1,530 with a male score), so the B runs retained more records (n = 25,692 at the community level). The B outputs are kept separate from the A analyses throughout (scripts/dale, results/dale, figures/pub/dale).


================================================================================
RESULTS
================================================================================

We report two colour metrics throughout: male colourfulness, modelled on the log scale, and sexual dichromatism, modelled in LociUVS units. Mean colourfulness and the dichromatism ratio behave like their respective counterparts and are given in the supplement. Every estimate carries its 95% credible interval. Alongside each we give a standardised effect size, written d, which is the estimate divided by the standard deviation of that response on the scale the model works in. That divisor is 0.431 for community male colourfulness and 31.9 LociUVS for community sexual dichromatism in Analysis 3, 1.11 for log relative abundance in Analysis 2a, 0.057 for the paired difference in Analysis 2b, and 0.457 and 34.4 LociUVS for the two species-level responses in Analysis 1. A coefficient and a contrast are different quantities, and we distinguish them. Coefficients hold every other predictor at its reference level, so a land-use coefficient in Analysis 3 describes tropical closed habitats specifically. Contrasts in Table 1 average the linear predictor over a balanced grid of the remaining factors, so they describe the model as a whole. Table 2 goes one step further and computes the same contrasts separately within each level of the other factor, which is what an interaction requires. Where these three views disagree, we say so, because the disagreement is usually the result.

Analysis 1: Phylogenetic association of colour with land use (A1b)

Plumage colour is strongly phylogenetically conserved. Phylogeny alone explained 86.4% [82.1, 90.1] of variance in male colourfulness, with a phylogenetic standard deviation of 0.563 against a residual of 0.174. Sexual dichromatism was less conserved, at 66.6% [58.6, 74.4], with 41.3 against 19.9. The ratio of the two standard deviations is 3.2 for colourfulness and 2.1 for the sex difference, so the magnitude of the sex difference is the more evolutionarily labile trait. Figure 3a and 3d show both traits mapped onto the tree.

No land-use association predicts colour once the scores are built from detections. Not one of the ten pairwise contrasts was credible for male colourfulness. The largest was pasture below cropland at -0.108 [-0.243, +0.028], d = -0.24, followed by pasture below primary vegetation at -0.085 [-0.193, +0.021], d = -0.19, each carrying a posterior probability of 0.94 that the difference is negative. Every contrast involving pasture runs negative and every contrast involving cropland runs positive, so the ordering that a credible result would have described is present in the point estimates. The intervals do not support it. Figure 3b shows the fitted lines against the species data and Figure 3c gives all ten contrasts.

Sexual dichromatism shows no land-use association either. Not one of its ten contrasts was credible, and the largest, pasture above primary vegetation at +6.82 LociUVS [-3.14, +16.84], d = 0.20, carries a posterior probability of only 0.90. Note that its sign is opposite to the colourfulness result: species associated with pasture are marginally duller and marginally more dimorphic, and neither margin is credible.

Zero scores are frequent and are not a defect. Seventy-six per cent of species score zero for pasture and 70% for cropland, but a zero records that the species was never detected in that land use, which is information rather than absence of it. The five proportions sum to 1 for every species, the design matrix is full rank once the intercept is dropped, and no identifiability problem arises. The real limitation is that a zero cannot distinguish a species that avoids a land use from one that no study sampled there, so these scores carry survey effort as well as ecology.

Unlike the row-based scores, these coefficients are not extrapolations. Each coefficient is the expected colour of a species detected only in that land use, at an association score of 1, and all five land uses now contain species at that corner: 128 for primary vegetation, 124 for secondary, 25 for plantation, 24 for cropland and 16 for pasture. Figure 3b still draws each fitted line solid across the observed range and dotted beyond it, but for these scores the dotted segments are short or absent.

Marginal credible intervals on the five coefficients would be uninformative here whatever the result. Because the five proportions sum to 1 per species, their coefficients are strongly correlated in the posterior, and each marginal interval spans roughly 0.8 units while the contrasts span 0.25. Comparing whether the marginal intervals overlap would return no result under either construction of the scores. Contrasting the draws is the only valid test, and we use it throughout Table 1 for the same reason.

The pasture result reported in earlier versions of this analysis was an artefact of counting absences. Building the association scores from every raw row rather than from detections (model A1, reported in the supplement) puts pasture credibly below primary vegetation at -0.147 [-0.273, -0.023]. That version of the score is substantially a measure of survey design: the two constructions correlate at 0.58 for primary vegetation and 0.79 for pasture, and 218 species shift their pasture score by more than 0.1. Under the detection-based scores the same contrast attenuates to -0.085 [-0.193, +0.021] and its posterior probability of being negative falls from 0.99 to 0.94. The sign is unchanged, so this is attenuation rather than reversal, but the effect is no longer distinguishable from zero. We report A1b and treat the row-based result as superseded.

A caveat applies to both constructions. Every species-level model returned a low energy Bayesian fraction of missing information, so the tails of these posteriors are not well characterised, and a contrast whose credibility rests on a 2.5% quantile is exactly the quantity such a warning bears on. This cuts in the same direction as the result: a marginally credible pasture effect under the row-based scores was resting on precisely the quantile the diagnostic questions. Neither version should be treated as settled until the model is refitted with a non-centred parameterisation of the phylogenetic term, or at higher adapt_delta with a longer warmup.

Analysis 2a: Colour as a predictor of relative abundance (A2c)

Colour does not predict how abundant a species is within its community. Neither colour main effect came close to credibility, at +0.011 [-0.036, +0.057], d = 0.01, for male colourfulness and -0.012 [-0.054, +0.030], d = -0.01, for sexual dichromatism. Not one of the eight colour by land-use interactions was credible either. These are well-estimated zeros rather than an absence of power. Bulk effective sample size was at least 846 for every parameter, and the intervals are narrow, for example male colourfulness in pasture at -0.017 [-0.082, +0.048].

One colour effect survives, and it is biome-level rather than land-use-level. More conspicuous males were credibly less abundant in tropical open communities, at -0.094 [-0.166, -0.017], d = -0.08. Because that term is a difference between slopes, the quantity to report is the slope itself. Averaged over land uses it is -0.091 [-0.172, -0.012] in tropical open habitats, against +0.003 [-0.045, +0.049] in tropical closed habitats and +0.006 [-0.087, +0.094] in temperate closed. One standard deviation of male colourfulness therefore multiplies a species' median relative abundance by 0.91 [0.84, 0.99] in tropical open habitats, and by nothing distinguishable from one in either forest biome. The term survives precisely because biome varies within a species, so the per-species phylogenetic effect cannot absorb it the way it absorbs a species-constant colour value.

What structures relative abundance is lineage and study, not colour. The model explained 53.2% [51.7, 54.7] of variance, and that variance sat almost entirely in the phylogenetic effect (sd 0.97) and the study effect (sd 0.95), above the residual (sd 0.65) and far above site (0.25) and block (0.20). Among land uses only pasture stood apart, at +0.295 [0.189, 0.409], d = 0.26, above primary vegetation and above every other modified type. Higher per-species relative abundance in pasture is what an impoverished and more dominated community looks like.

Analysis 2b: Colour and abundance change following land-use conversion (A2d)

Colour barely moves how much abundance a species gains or loses when its habitat is converted, but the effect it does have is positive and consistent. Neither colour coefficient was credible, and only one colour by land-use interaction reached credibility, male colourfulness in plantation at +0.0018 [+0.0004, +0.0032], which at d = 0.03 is negligible. The colour coefficient is the slope in tropical closed habitats specifically, however, and it is the only biome where the slope is flat, at -0.0006 [-0.0041, +0.0028]. Averaged over biomes the slope is credibly positive in every land use, from +0.0045 [+0.0009, +0.0083] in secondary vegetation to +0.0063 [+0.0026, +0.0102] in plantation, with effect sizes from 0.08 to 0.11. Sexual dichromatism behaves the same way, from +0.0041 to +0.0054. More conspicuous species therefore lose slightly less abundance under conversion than drab ones, by an amount that is real, uniform across land uses, and small.

Biome, not land use, is what modulates the colour effect. Reading the slope within each biome gives +0.0094 [+0.0031, +0.0156], d = 0.17, in temperate closed habitats and +0.0079 [+0.0027, +0.0132], d = 0.14, in tropical open habitats, against nothing in tropical closed. Species with larger sex differences behave the same way, at +0.0122 [+0.0077, +0.0167], d = 0.22, in tropical open habitats. These are the largest colour effects anywhere in the study and each still amounts to roughly a fifth of the residual standard deviation. Figure 5a and 5d give the colour slope within each biome and within each land use, and Figure 5c and 5f show the observed change split by biome and colour tercile, which is the raw form of this result.

What does structure abundance change is lineage. The model explained 23.1% [22.5, 23.7] of variance, almost all of it carried by the phylogenetic random effect (sd 0.072), which exceeded the residual (sd 0.050) and dwarfed study (sd 0.015), block (sd 0.0011), and site (sd 0.0002). Related species gain and lose abundance together under conversion, and they do so for reasons that colour does not capture. Among land uses, cropland lost more than plantation, at -0.0041 [-0.0068, -0.0015], d = -0.07, and pasture lost less than cropland, at +0.0033 [+0.0004, +0.0059], d = 0.06. Species in tropical open habitats fared better than those in temperate closed, at +0.021 [+0.001, +0.040], d = 0.37.

Read alongside Analysis 2a, the two abundance results agree. Colour does not predict a species' standing share of its community, and it does not predict how that share changes when the habitat is converted. Note the sign contrast on tropical open habitats, where more conspicuous males held a lower standing share yet lost marginally less against their own baseline. These are different questions, one about level and one about change, so the two are not in conflict.

Analysis 3: Community plumage colour (A3b, A3a)

Land-use conversion dulls male plumage in tropical closed, by a small and consistent margin. All four modified land uses fell below primary vegetation: cropland -0.081 [-0.106, -0.056], d = -0.19, pasture -0.082 [-0.132, -0.034], d = -0.19, plantation -0.050 [-0.069, -0.031], d = -0.12, and secondary vegetation -0.035 [-0.055, -0.015], d = -0.08. On the response scale these are losses of 7.8%, 7.9%, 4.9% and 3.4% in male colourfulness. Because neither trophic niche nor body mass interacts with land use, these coefficients are also exactly the land-use contrasts computed within tropical closed, which is how they appear in Figure 6a.

Averaging over biome and trophic guild changes which of those effects survive. Cropland remains the clearest, at -0.067 [-0.093, -0.042], d = -0.15, and secondary vegetation and plantation hold at -0.035 [-0.050, -0.019] and -0.025 [-0.045, -0.005]. Pasture does not survive, at -0.028 [-0.065, +0.010], d = -0.06, with a posterior probability of 0.92 that it is negative. The pasture coefficient is large only in tropical closed, because both biome interactions with pasture run positive, and averaging over biomes cancels most of it. We therefore report cropland, not pasture, as the land use that most reliably dulls bird communities.

Cropland is also the only land use that separates from the other modified types. It sat below secondary vegetation at -0.032 [-0.060, -0.005] and below plantation at -0.042 [-0.072, -0.011]. No other pair of modified land uses differed credibly, so the four converted habitats are otherwise indistinguishable in male colourfulness. Table 1 gives all ten pairwise contrasts.

Computing those contrasts separately within each biome shows that the effect of land use is not general. It belongs to tropical closed habitats. Within tropical closed habitats, cropland was in addition credibly below secondary vegetation at -0.046 [-0.072, -0.019] and below plantation at -0.031 [-0.057, -0.003]. In temperate closed habitats not one of the ten land-use contrasts was credible. In tropical open habitats only two were, secondary vegetation at -0.046 [-0.071, -0.019] and cropland at -0.081 [-0.130, -0.032], with pasture flat at +0.011 [-0.071, +0.093]. The community-level dulling is therefore driven by tropical closed, holds partially in tropical open habitats, and is absent from temperate closed. Figure 6a and 6d group the contrasts by biome, and Figure 6c and 6f show the same comparison in the observed distributions.

Cropland acts in opposite directions on dichromatism depending on the biome. In tropical closed habitats it lowered the sex difference by -2.64 LociUVS [-4.52, -0.79], d = -0.08, and in tropical open habitats it raised it by +5.68 [1.81, 9.44], d = 0.18. The interaction contrast confirms that the two are genuinely different rather than merely differing in precision, at +8.31 [4.02, 12.53], d = 0.26. Averaging across biomes, as Table 1 does, cancels most of this and leaves cropland looking unremarkable. This is the clearest case in the study where a single averaged number would mislead.

Sexual dichromatism barely responds to land use at all. Only cropland moved it as a coefficient, at -2.64 LociUVS [-4.52, -0.79], d = -0.08, and only secondary vegetation survived as a contrast, at -1.48 [-2.57, -0.32], d = -0.05. Both are small against a response whose standard deviation is 31.9 LociUVS. Overall colourfulness and the size of the sex difference therefore respond to conversion in different ways, and the sex difference barely responds at all.

Biome matters more than land use, and it pulls colour and dichromatism in opposite directions. Temperate forest communities carried duller males than tropical closed ones, at -0.136 [-0.237, -0.042], d = -0.31, yet larger sex differences, at +7.11 LociUVS [1.26, 12.83], d = 0.22. Both survive averaging, at -0.102 [-0.203, -0.008] and +5.87 [0.06, 11.59]. Tropical open communities sat below temperate closed ones on sexual dichromatism, at -8.91 [-16.99, -0.68], d = -0.28, the largest biome contrast in this analysis. This dissociation between how colourful a community is and how far its sexes differ is the clearest structural result in the analysis.

Body mass and trophic guild are the strongest predictors in the model. A one standard deviation increase in log body mass lowered male colourfulness by -0.136 [-0.142, -0.131], d = -0.32, and sexual dichromatism by -5.02 LociUVS [-5.43, -4.63], d = -0.16. Both intervals are narrow, which is what 24,614 records buy. Invertivores were duller than the aquatic predator reference, at -0.179 [-0.294, -0.063], d = -0.42, and markedly less dichromatic, at -18.72 [-26.75, -10.77], d = -0.59. Nectarivores carried the largest single effect anywhere in the analysis, a sexual dichromatism difference of +22.84 LociUVS [14.59, 31.27], d = 0.72, without being credibly more colourful. Granivores were also more dichromatic, at +8.68 [0.49, 16.79], d = 0.27.

Biome by land-use interactions are few and modest, and they concentrate in sexual dichromatism. Conversion in tropical open habitats raised the sex difference above what the main effects predict, in cropland at +8.30 LociUVS [4.02, 12.53], d = 0.26, and in plantation at +3.14 [0.59, 5.78], d = 0.10. Secondary vegetation in temperate closed habitats ran the other way, at -3.18 [-6.05, -0.35], d = -0.10. For male colourfulness only tropical open plantation reached credibility, and it sits on the boundary at +0.035 [0.000, 0.071], d = 0.08.

Adding trophic guild and body mass roughly doubles what the model explains. Bayesian R2 rose from 7.0% [6.5, 7.5] to 15.1% [14.4, 15.8] for male colourfulness, and from 7.6% [7.0, 8.2] to 18.1% [17.3, 18.9] for sexual dichromatism. Guild-specific and size-specific responses to land use earn their place beyond that. Leave-one-out cross-validation favoured A3a over A3b by 220 units of expected log predictive density (SE 28) for male colourfulness and 248 (SE 32) for sexual dichromatism, each roughly eight standard errors.

Three-way sensitivity checks (A2e, A2f)

The three-way colour by biome by land-use interaction earns no place in either abundance model. For relative abundance (A2e) the three-way model was worse than the reported two-way model by 4.4 units of expected log predictive density (SE 3.2), and for the paired difference (A2f) by 1.3 units (SE 3.7). Neither difference reaches two standard errors, so the two models are statistically indistinguishable and parsimony selects the two-way form. We therefore report A2c and A2d throughout. Both checks used male colourfulness.

Dale-colour replication (B analyses)

The community-level effect of land use on colour does not replicate under Dale scores. Not one land-use coefficient was credible for Dale male colourfulness, at +0.007 [-0.003, +0.016] for cropland, +0.014 [-0.005, +0.033] for pasture, +0.000 [-0.007, +0.007] for plantation, and -0.003 [-0.010, +0.005] for secondary vegetation. Every Cooney estimate is negative and credible, and every Dale estimate is near zero or positive. The Dale sexual dichromatism was likewise flat across all four land uses. Dale scores also carry much less land-use-linked signal for colourfulness, with Bayesian R2 of 9.1% [8.4, 9.8] for male colourfulness against the Cooney value of 15.1%. For sexual dichromatism the position reverses, at 19.4% [18.7, 20.2] against Cooney's 18.1%.

What does replicate is the model architecture and the body-size story. Leave-one-out favoured B3a over B3b for all four Dale responses, by 97 to 158 units of expected log predictive density (SE 16 to 25), so guild-specific and size-specific land-use responses earn their place under either colour system. The mass by land-use interactions were credible in matching directions under both, positive in pasture and negative in plantation and secondary vegetation. That corroboration matters, because these are the interactions whose sign changed when the primary-vegetation filter was corrected, and Dale scores reproduce the corrected pattern. The trophic interactions do not replicate at all. Under Dale scores not one land use by trophic niche interaction was credible on any metric, and the Cooney headline of frugivores duller in plantation is absent, at -0.075 [-0.212, +0.066] for Dale male colourfulness against a credible Cooney value of -0.286.

Colour predicts relative abundance no better under Dale scores, with one exception. B2c reproduced the A2c structure closely, at 53.7% [52.2, 55.1] of variance explained, carried by phylogeny (sd 0.98) and study (sd 0.95) rather than colour, and with the same credible negative effect of male colourfulness in tropical open habitats at -0.082 [-0.144, -0.018] against Cooney's -0.094. Two independently constructed colour metrics therefore give the same tropical open result, which is the strongest replication in the study. The exception is a credible positive interaction between male colourfulness and cropland, at +0.054 [+0.015, +0.093], with no Cooney counterpart. Set against sixteen colour terms across the two models it is a lone boundary result, and we do not read it as evidence that colour predicts abundance in cropland.

B2d reproduced A2d in every respect that matters. Bayesian R2 was 23.2% [22.6, 23.8] against A2d's 23.1%, the variance was again dominated by phylogeny (sd 0.069) above the residual (sd 0.049) and far above study (sd 0.014), and the credible colour terms were again of trivial magnitude. Both colour systems give the same answer: abundance change is phylogenetically structured, and colour does not predict it.

The species-level result does not replicate. B1 showed strong phylogenetic conservatism as A1 did. However, contrasting the posterior draws against primary vegetation gives no pasture effect under Dale scores, at +0.028 [-0.014, +0.072] for male colourfulness, of the opposite sign to Cooney and not credible. Neither Dale metric produced a credible contrast against primary vegetation on any land use. We therefore regard the pasture result of Analysis 1 as specific to the Cooney ultraviolet-sensitive metrics and not established.

Summary of the replication

Two of the four analyses reinforce the Cooney results under an independent colour-scoring system, and two do not. The two abundance analyses agree closely, both in the variance structure and in the conclusion that colour predicts neither abundance nor its change, and the tropical open effect on male colourfulness replicates almost exactly. The species-level analysis agrees that colour is strongly phylogenetically conserved.

The divergences all concern the effect of land use on colour itself. The community-level dulling of Analysis 3 is credible under Cooney metrics and absent under Dale scores. The trophic interactions that dominate Analysis 3 under Cooney metrics are likewise entirely absent. The pasture association of Analysis 1 does not reproduce, and no Dale contrast replaces it. Each divergence sits where the two scoring systems would be expected to differ most, since they weight plumage patches and ultraviolet reflectance differently. We report the Cooney results as primary and treat every land-use effect on colour as metric-dependent until a third scoring system arbitrates.
