METHODS AND RESULTS
Draft: avian plumage colour and land-use change
================================================================================
Four analyses (community colour, colour-abundance, colour-abundance change, and a phylogenetic test). Each is run twice: once with Cooney UVS colour (the A analyses, primary) and once with the independent Dale plumage scores (the B analyses, a colour-source robustness check), reported in separate sections. Model codes in parentheses map to scripts and ANALYSIS_NOTES.md.

STATUS, 2026-08-11. The methods below describe the corrected dataset and the current model specifications. No model has been fitted against them yet, so the results section is a placeholder. Every number previously reported here came from a dataset built by a faulty filter and has been removed rather than carried forward.

METHODS
================================================================================

Data

We combined the PREDICTS database (site-level bird survey records from studies spanning multiple land-use types worldwide) with Cooney et al.'s spectrophotometric plumage measurements (ultraviolet-sensitive [UVS] visual model, LociUVS scores) and AVONET traits (body mass, trophic niche). The merged dataset comprised 216,834 records from 1,703 passerine species. Our primary (A) analyses used the Cooney UVS model. We derived four species-level colour variables: mean colourfulness (mean of male and female LociUVS), male conspicuousness (male LociUVS), the sexual dichromatism ratio (male over female LociUVS), and the sexual dichromatism difference (male minus female LociUVS, which can be negative). We then repeated every analysis with the independent Dale et al. (2015) plumage scores in place of the UVS metrics, as a colour-source robustness check reported separately (the B analyses, see Dale-colour replication).

We collapsed the WWF biome field into three categories (Biome4): Tropical Forest (moist and dry broadleaf and coniferous forests, the reference), Tropical Open (tropical grasslands, savannas, shrublands, and mangroves), and Temperate Forest (temperate broadleaf, mixed and conifer forests, and Mediterranean forests, woodlands and scrub). A fourth category, Temperate Open (temperate and montane grasslands, tundra), was excluded. It contributed 115 primary-vegetation records against 36, 42, 0 and 35 records in the four modified land uses, so its biome by land-use interaction was not estimable and no collapse of those cells left a defensible contrast. Excluding it cost 0.9% of records. Land use (five levels) was Primary vegetation (reference), Secondary, Plantation forest, Cropland, and Pasture. Body mass was log-transformed and standardised (z_logMass). Trophic niche (AVONET) entered as a fixed factor (Aquatic predator reference).

Site-level analyses used records with non-zero abundance, in the retained biomes, from publications containing primary vegetation plus at least one other land use (n = 25,864 records, 1,420 species, "present.csv"). The biome exclusion preceded the primary-vegetation filter, so each publication was judged on the records that enter the models. Land-use representation was Primary vegetation 10,805, Plantation forest 6,482, Secondary 5,733, Cropland 2,225, and Pasture 619. All fifteen biome by land-use cells were estimable, the thinnest being Tropical Open by Pasture at 96 records from 34 species. Pasture is nonetheless sparse, because one contributing publication holding 1,387 pasture records contained no primary vegetation and was therefore excluded, and we interpret pasture effects with corresponding caution.

All models used the canonical PREDICTS nested random structure: study, block within study, and site within block (SS, SSB, SSBS, 40 studies / 203 blocks / 2,335 sites), which is fully nested because these identifiers are hierarchically coded. Analyses 2 to 4 additionally included a phylogenetic random effect from the BigBirdTree tree (BBtree2, 9,072 tips, Jetz taxonomy) as a Brownian-motion covariance matrix A (ape::vcv.phylo, correlation form). Species were matched to tips in three stages (jetz_sp, then Best_guess_binomial, then a curated genus-synonym table), with no grafting, and tips claimed by more than one species were dropped. Matching retained 1,157 species (19,785 records) for Analysis 2, 1,154 species (50,534 records) for Analysis 3, and 1,391 species (1,254 with Cooney colour data) at the species level.

Statistical framework

Models were fitted in brms/CmdStan. Colourfulness and dichromatism-ratio responses, and relative abundance, used a lognormal family. The dichromatism difference and the paired abundance difference used a Gaussian family. Effects are termed credible when the 95% posterior credible interval (CI) excludes zero. Bayesian R2 is reported as posterior median [95% CI].

Analysis 1: Community plumage colour across biomes and land uses (A1b, A1a)

For each of the four colour responses (community-weighted over occurrence records, n = 24,614 with non-missing colour), we fitted two nested models that share the same data, priors, sampler, and random structure. The primary model (A1b) was

    colour ~ Biome4 * Predominant_simple + Trophic.Niche + z_logMass
             + (1 | SS) + (1 | SSB) + (1 | SSBS)

We ran A1b in two variants, a base model with biome by land use only and a covariate model that adds trophic niche and body mass as main effects, and we report the covariate model. The extension (A1a) adds two interaction families to the A1b covariate model, land use by trophic niche and land use by body mass:

    colour ~ Biome4 * Predominant_simple + Trophic.Niche + z_logMass
             + Predominant_simple:z_logMass + Predominant_simple:Trophic.Niche
             + (1 | SS) + (1 | SSB) + (1 | SSBS)

A1a is therefore a nested superset of A1b, and we compared the two by leave-one-out cross-validation (LOO) to test whether the guild-specific and size-specific interactions earn their place. Priors were Normal(0,2) on the intercept, Normal(0,0.5) on fixed effects, and Exponential(2) on the standard deviations and sigma for the lognormal responses, and Normal(0,50), Normal(0,20), Exponential(2) for the Gaussian response. We ran 4 chains for 3,000 iterations (1,500 warmup) at adapt_delta = 0.90. No phylogenetic term was included. Because colour is a species-level constant with no within-species variance, a per-species phylogenetic intercept is not identifiable at the record level, where it absorbs all between-species variance and collapses the residual and site terms. The phylogenetic question is addressed at the species level in Analysis 4.

Analysis 2: Colour as a predictor of relative abundance (A2c)

We tested whether colour predicts a species' share of its community, and whether that share depends on biome or land use. Relative abundance was each species' effort-corrected count divided by its site (SSBS) total, and each colour metric was standardised. For male conspicuousness and the dichromatism difference we fitted (lognormal)

    relative_abundance ~ z_colour * Biome4 + z_colour * Predominant_simple
                         + Biome4 * Predominant_simple
                         + (1|SS) + (1|SSB) + (1|SSBS) + (1 | gr(phylo, cov = A))

that is, all three two-way interactions but no three-way term. Priors were as in Analysis 1 (lognormal), with 4 chains by 2,000 iterations (1,000 warmup) at adapt_delta = 0.90. Because colour, trophic niche and body mass are themselves phylogenetically conserved, the colour main effect is interpreted as an effect beyond phylogeny. As a sensitivity check we also fitted the full three-way interaction (model A2e) and compared it to the two-way model by LOO.

Analysis 3: Colour and abundance change following land-use conversion (A2d)

To include absences and anchor each species to its own baseline, we computed, per study, each species' mean relative abundance across primary-vegetation sites, and subtracted it from that species' relative abundance at each modified site (the paired difference). Exact-zero differences, meaning species absent from both, were dropped as uninformative. This yielded 66,225 non-zero differences, of which 54,038 were negative. Primary vegetation is thus absorbed into the response, leaving four land-use levels. For the same two colour metrics we fitted the same two-way structure as Analysis 2 with a Gaussian family (n = 50,534). Priors were Normal(0,0.5), Normal(0,0.2), and Exponential(5), and we ran 4 chains for 3,500 iterations (1,500 warmup) at adapt_delta = 0.95, max_treedepth = 12. The three-way interaction was again fitted as a sensitivity check (model A2f) and compared by LOO.

Analysis 4: Phylogenetic association of colour with land use (A3)

At the species level, we regressed each colour response on the within-species proportion of records in each land use (five compositional proportions summing to 1), using a no-intercept parameterisation so each coefficient is the expected colour for a species recorded exclusively in that land use:

    colour ~ 0 + prop_Cropland + prop_Pasture + prop_Plantation_forest
                + prop_Primary_vegetation + prop_Secondary + (1 | gr(phylo, cov = A))

These proportions were computed from the full raw dataset rather than the filtered analytical dataset, because a species' habitat association is a property of the species and is best estimated from all of its records. Priors were broadened for species-level data: Normal(0,5) on fixed effects for the lognormal responses, Normal(0,50) for the Gaussian response, Exponential(2) on the standard deviations and sigma for the lognormal responses, and Exponential(0.05) for the Gaussian response. We ran 4 chains for 4,000 iterations (2,000 warmup) at adapt_delta = 0.95, max_treedepth = 12, for n = 1,254 species. Because the compositional coefficients are strongly correlated in the posterior, we compare land uses by posterior contrast rather than by inspecting whether marginal credible intervals overlap.

Dale-colour replication (B analyses)

To test whether the findings depend on the colour-scoring system, we repeated all four analyses with Dale et al.'s plumage scores in place of the Cooney UVS metrics, holding the data, priors, sampler, random structure, and metric families identical. These are the B analyses: B1b and B1a replicate A1b and A1a, B2c replicates A2c, B2d replicates A2d, and B3 replicates A3. From each colour system we derived the same four responses (mean colourfulness, male conspicuousness, the dichromatism ratio, and the dichromatism difference), with the same lognormal and Gaussian families. Dale scores cover more species than the UVS metrics (1,679 against 1,530 with a male score), so the B runs retained more records (n = 25,692 at the community level). The B outputs are kept separate from the A analyses throughout (scripts/dale, results/dale, figures/pub/dale).
