METHODS AND RESULTS
Draft: avian plumage colour and land-use change
================================================================================
Four analyses (community colour, colour-abundance, colour-abundance change, and a
phylogenetic test). Model codes in parentheses map to scripts and ANALYSIS_NOTES.md.

METHODS
================================================================================

Data

We combined the PREDICTS database (site-level bird survey records from studies
spanning multiple land-use types worldwide) with Cooney et al.'s spectrophotometric
plumage measurements (ultraviolet-sensitive [UVS] visual model, LociUVS scores) and
AVONET traits (body mass, trophic niche). The merged dataset comprised 216,834
records from 1,703 passerine species. All colour metrics used the UVS model (Dale
scores excluded). We derived four species-level colour variables: mean colourfulness
(mean of male and female LociUVS), male conspicuousness (male LociUVS), the sexual
dichromatism ratio (male / female LociUVS), and the sexual dichromatism difference
(male minus female LociUVS, which can be negative).

Site-level analyses used records from studies containing primary vegetation plus at
least one other land use (n = 34,653, "present.csv"). Land use (five levels) was
Primary vegetation (reference), Secondary, Plantation forest, Cropland, and Pasture.
We collapsed the original WWF biome field into four categories (Biome4): Tropical
Forest (moist/dry broadleaf and coniferous forests, reference), Tropical Open
(tropical grasslands/savannas/shrublands, mangroves), Temperate Forest (temperate
broadleaf/mixed and conifer forests, and Mediterranean forests/woodlands/scrub), and
Temperate Open (temperate and montane grasslands, tundra). Body mass was
log-transformed and standardised (z_logMass). Trophic niche (AVONET) entered as a
fixed factor (Aquatic predator reference).

All models used the canonical PREDICTS nested random structure: study, block within
study, and site within block (SS, SSB, SSBS, 59 studies / 361 blocks / 3,716 sites),
which is fully nested because these identifiers are hierarchically coded. Analyses 2 to
4 additionally included a phylogenetic random effect from the BigBirdTree tree (BBtree2,
9,072 tips, Jetz taxonomy) as a Brownian-motion covariance matrix A (ape::vcv.phylo,
correlation form). Species were matched to tips in three stages (jetz_sp, then
Best_guess_binomial, then a curated genus-synonym table), with no grafting. Three
BirdLife names mapping to a single tip were dropped. Matching retained 1,348 of 1,681
species (26,951 records, 77.8%) in present.csv, and 1,391 species (1,254 with colour
data) at the species level.

Statistical framework

Models were fitted in brms/CmdStan. Colourfulness and dichromatism-ratio responses,
and relative abundance, used a lognormal family. The dichromatism difference and the
paired abundance difference used a Gaussian family. Effects are termed credible when
the 95% posterior credible interval (CI) excludes zero. Bayesian R2 is reported as
posterior median [95% CI].

Analysis 1: Community plumage colour across biomes and land uses (A1b, A1a)

For each of the four colour responses (community-weighted over occurrence records,
n = 33,210 with non-missing colour), we fitted two nested models that share the same
data, priors, sampler, and random structure. The primary model (A1b) was

    colour ~ Biome4 * Predominant_simple + Trophic.Niche + z_logMass
             + (1 | SS) + (1 | SSB) + (1 | SSBS)

We ran A1b in two variants, a base model with biome x land use only and a covariate
model that adds trophic niche and body mass as main effects, and we report the
covariate model. The extension (A1a) adds two interaction families to the A1b
covariate model, land use x trophic niche and land use x body mass:

    colour ~ Biome4 * Predominant_simple + Trophic.Niche + z_logMass
             + Predominant_simple:z_logMass + Predominant_simple:Trophic.Niche
             + (1 | SS) + (1 | SSB) + (1 | SSBS)

A1a is therefore a nested superset of A1b, and we compared the two by leave-one-out
cross-validation (LOO) to test whether the guild- and size-specific interactions earn
their place. Priors were Normal(0,2) on the intercept, Normal(0,0.5) on fixed effects,
and Exponential(2) on the standard deviations and sigma for the lognormal responses,
and Normal(0,50), Normal(0,20), Exponential(2) for the Gaussian response. We ran 4
chains for 3,000 iterations (1,500 warmup) at adapt_delta = 0.90. No phylogenetic term
was included. Because colour is a species-level constant with no within-species
variance, a per-species phylogenetic intercept is not identifiable at the record level,
where it absorbs all between-species variance and collapses the residual and site
terms. The phylogenetic question is addressed at the species level in Analysis 4. All
models converged with no divergences. For A1b the maximum Rhat was 1.017 and the
minimum bulk effective sample size (ESS) was 467. For A1a the maximum Rhat was 1.01 and
the minimum bulk ESS was 846.

Analysis 2: Colour as a predictor of relative abundance (A2c)

We tested whether colour predicts a species' share of its community, and whether that
depends on biome or land use. Relative abundance was each species' effort-corrected
count divided by its site (SSBS) total, and each colour metric was standardised. For
male conspicuousness and the dichromatism difference we fitted (lognormal)

    relative_abundance ~ z_colour * Biome4 + z_colour * Predominant_simple
                         + Biome4 * Predominant_simple
                         + (1|SS) + (1|SSB) + (1|SSBS) + (1 | gr(phylo, cov = A))

i.e. all three two-way interactions but no three-way term. Priors were as in Analysis 1
(lognormal), with 4 chains x 2,000 iterations (1,000 warmup) at adapt_delta = 0.90.
Because colour and trophic/mass are themselves phylogenetically conserved, the colour
main effect is interpreted as an effect beyond phylogeny. As a sensitivity check we also
fitted the full three-way interaction (model A2e) and compared it to the two-way model
by LOO. Both models converged (0 divergences, maximum Rhat <= 1.005, minimum bulk
ESS >= 1,003).

Analysis 3: Colour and abundance change following land-use conversion (A2d)

To include absences and anchor each species to its own baseline, we computed, per
study, each species' mean relative abundance across primary-vegetation sites, and
subtracted it from that species' relative abundance at each modified site (the paired
difference). Exact-zero differences, meaning species absent from both, were dropped as
uninformative. Primary vegetation is thus absorbed into the response (four land-use
levels remain). For the same two colour metrics we fitted the same two-way structure
as Analysis 2 with a Gaussian family (n = 50,972 for male conspicuousness). Priors were
Normal(0,0.5), Normal(0,0.2), and Exponential(5), and we ran 4 chains for 3,500
iterations (1,500 warmup) at adapt_delta = 0.95, max_treedepth = 12. The three-way
interaction was again fitted as a sensitivity check (model A2f) and compared by LOO.
Models converged with no divergences. The maximum Rhat (1.015) was elevated only on the
global intercept, with all colour, biome, and land-use terms at Rhat <= 1.01.

Analysis 4: Phylogenetic association of colour with land use (A3)

At the species level, we regressed each colour response on the within-species
proportion of records in each land use (five compositional proportions summing to 1),
using a no-intercept parameterisation so each coefficient is the expected colour for a
species recorded exclusively in that land use:

    colour ~ 0 + prop_Cropland + prop_Pasture + prop_Plantation_forest
                + prop_Primary_vegetation + prop_Secondary + (1 | gr(phylo, cov = A))

Priors were broadened for species-level data: Normal(0,5) fixed effects for the
lognormal responses, Normal(0,100) for the Gaussian response, and Exponential(2) on SDs
and sigma. We ran 4 chains for 4,000 iterations (2,000 warmup) at adapt_delta = 0.95,
max_treedepth = 12, for n = 1,254 species. All models converged (0 divergences, maximum
Rhat <= 1.01), though the dichromatism difference showed low E-BFMI and is interpreted
cautiously.


================================================================================
RESULTS
================================================================================

Analysis 1: Community plumage colour (A1b, A1a)

Community colour was shaped mainly by biome, body mass, and trophic niche, with a
modest land-use effect. All four modified land uses held credibly less colourful
communities than primary vegetation (mean colourfulness: Cropland -0.08, Pasture
-0.08, Plantation -0.05, Secondary -0.04 on the log scale, with male conspicuousness
comparable), but no land use shifted dichromatism on its own. Non-tropical-forest
biomes were less colourful (Temperate Forest and Temperate Open each about -0.14 to
-0.17 for mean and male colour, and Tropical Open credibly lower for male colour), yet
biome and dichromatism dissociated. Temperate Forest communities were credibly *more*
sexually dichromatic (ratio +0.07, difference +5.2 LociUVS units) while Tropical Open
were less (ratio -0.04, difference -6.2). Larger-bodied birds were consistently less
colourful and less dichromatic (mean -0.11, male -0.12, ratio -0.023, difference -3.9).
Among guilds, nectarivores stood out as both the most colourful and by far the most
dichromatic (colour +0.17, difference +27.5), whereas invertivores were less colourful
(male -0.13) and, with omnivores and frugivores, less dichromatic. Biome x land-use
interactions were mostly modest. The clearest were that plantation and cropland
communities in Tropical Open biomes were more dichromatic than expected (difference
+3.0 to +6.5), and Temperate Forest plantations slightly more colourful than the biome
and land-use main effects alone predict. Bayesian R2 was 16.8% (mean colour), 15.0%
(male), 11.0% (dichromatism ratio), and 15.6% (dichromatism difference). The base
(biome x land-use only) models explained roughly half as much, confirming that trophic
niche and body mass carry much of the colour signal.

Allowing land use to interact with trophic niche and body mass (A1a) resolves guild-
and size-specific responses that the community-average model cannot show, and LOO
favours A1a over A1b for all four responses (expected log predictive density higher by
294 to 419, each about 9 to 12 standard errors). Bayesian R2 rises accordingly, from
16.8 to 18.7 percent for mean colour, 15.0 to 17.1 for male colour, 11.0 to 13.2 for
the dichromatism ratio, and 15.6 to 17.5 for the difference. The clearest guild effect
is that frugivores are far less colourful in plantation forest than the main effects
predict (mean colour -0.29 [-0.55, -0.03], male colour -0.31 [-0.58, -0.03]). Sexual
dichromatism responds by guild in the opposite direction to colourfulness. The large
male-female difference of nectarivores collapses in pasture (-18.1 [-33.4, -3.4]) and
secondary vegetation (-15.7 [-28.6, -3.1]), whereas frugivores become more dichromatic
in pasture (+20.7 [+5.9, +35.1]) and nectarivores more dichromatic in plantation forest
on the ratio scale (+0.20 [+0.01, +0.38]). Body size filters colour by land use as
well. The usual pattern of larger birds being duller and less dichromatic weakens in
pasture and cropland (for male colour, +0.06 and +0.03 per standard deviation of log
mass) and strengthens for dichromatism in plantation forest and secondary vegetation.
Because these interactions absorb part of the land-use signal, A1a's biome x land-use
terms shift relative to A1b, so we report biome x land use from A1b and the guild and
size interactions from A1a.

Analysis 2: Colour as a predictor of relative abundance (A2c)

Relative abundance was strongly structured by phylogeny and study (sd(phylo) = 0.91,
sd(study) = 0.95, Bayesian R2 = 51%), but essentially not by colour. Neither colour
main effect was credible, and, critically, no colour x land-use interaction was
credible for either metric. The only surviving colour effect was at the biome level:
more conspicuous males were credibly less relatively abundant in Tropical Open
communities (-0.089 [-0.147, -0.030]). Land-use and biome x land-use terms reflected
community composition rather than colour (e.g. higher relative abundance in Pasture,
+0.24, and Temperate Open x Cropland +0.56).

This contrasts sharply with a simpler model of colour x land use alone (without biome
or the phylogenetic and full study/site structure), which returned several credible
colour x land-use interactions (e.g. male colour and dichromatism difference higher in
Pasture, +0.07 and +0.10, and colourful species scarcer in Plantation, -0.03). None of
these survived the addition of biome and phylogeny, indicating they were confounded
with biome distribution and shared ancestry. The full three-way colour x biome x
land-use interaction (A2e) was not favoured by LOO. It was credibly worse than the
two-way model (ELPD difference -10.9 +/- 4.2) and contained no credible three-way
term, so we report the two-way model.

Analysis 3: Colour and abundance change following conversion (A2d)

When abundance change relative to each species' primary-vegetation baseline was
modelled (absences included), colour again explained little. Bayesian R2 was 22.7%,
but this was driven almost entirely by the phylogenetic term (sd(phylo) = 0.072 vs.
residual sigma = 0.050): abundance change carries lineage-level structure, not a colour
signal. Colour main effects and all colour x land-use interactions were not credible. A
few colour x biome interactions reached credibility but were negligible in magnitude
(on the order of 0.01 on the +/-0.06 difference scale, e.g. male colour x Tropical
Open +0.009, dichromatism difference x Tropical Open +0.011). The three-way sensitivity
model (A2f) was statistically indistinguishable from the two-way model by LOO (ELPD
difference -3.4 +/- 4.5) and again yielded no robust three-way effect. We report the
two-way model.

Analysis 4: Phylogenetic association of colour with land use (A3)

Plumage colour was strongly phylogenetically conserved: Bayesian R2 was 88.6%
(mean colour) and 86.2% (male), with phylogenetic SDs far exceeding residuals.
Dichromatism was less conserved (ratio 46.6%, difference 65.6%). Because phylogenetic
variance dominated, land-use proportion coefficients had wide, overlapping CIs, but a
consistent pattern emerged for colourfulness: species associated with Pasture had the
lowest expected colour (mean 4.42, male 4.44 on the log scale) and those associated
with Cropland the highest (4.67, 4.68), a Pasture-Primary gap of about -0.15. No
land-use association credibly predicted either dichromatism metric.

Synthesis

Plumage colour is a strongly phylogenetically conserved species trait (Analysis 4), and
at the community level it is shaped more by biome, trophic ecology, and body mass than
by land use. Modified land uses do hold modestly less colourful communities, and colour
and dichromatism respond to biome in opposite directions (Analysis 1b). When land use
is allowed to interact with guild and body size (Analysis 1a), a sharper filter appears.
Frugivores are much duller in plantation forest, the strong sexual dichromatism of
nectarivores erodes in pasture and secondary vegetation, and the size dependence of
colour weakens in open land uses. These guild- and size-specific effects improve
prediction over the community-average model, yet the main message holds. Land use
reshapes colour through which guilds and body sizes it favours, not through a uniform
shift. Once biome and shared ancestry are accounted for, colour does not
predict a species' relative abundance across land uses (Analysis 2): the colour x
land-use associations seen in simpler models are confounded artefacts, and the only
robust colour-abundance signal is biome-level (conspicuous males are scarcer in
tropical-open communities). Nor does colour predict how much abundance a species gains
or loses under land-use conversion (Analysis 3). That change is governed by lineage
and study structure rather than colour. Together, the analyses provide little support
for plumage colour itself acting as a filter on how passerine communities respond to
land-use change, once the biome context and evolutionary non-independence of colour
are properly controlled.
