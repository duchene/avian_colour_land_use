METHODS AND RESULTS
Draft — avian plumage colour and land-use change
================================================================================

METHODS
================================================================================

Data

We used a dataset combining the PREDICTS database (site-level bird survey records
from studies spanning multiple land-use types worldwide) with Cooney et al.'s
spectrophotometric plumage measurements (ultraviolet-sensitive [UVS] visual model
LociUVS scores) and AVONET life-history traits (body mass, trophic niche, habitat
affiliation). The merged dataset comprised 216,834 records from 1,703 passerine
species. All plumage colour metrics were derived from the UVS visual model (LociUVS);
Dale-model scores were excluded. We constructed four species-level colour variables:
(i) mean colourfulness (meancolcooney): the mean of male and female LociUVS;
(ii) male conspicuousness (malecolcooney): male LociUVS alone; (iii) sexual
dichromatism ratio (dichrocooney): male LociUVS divided by female LociUVS; and
(iv) sexual dichromatism difference (dichrodiff): male minus female LociUVS (an
arithmetic difference that can be negative when females are more colourful than
males).

For all site-level analyses (Analyses 1 and 2), we filtered to passerines with
non-zero abundance from studies that included primary vegetation alongside at least
one other land-use type, yielding 34,653 records. The land-use variable
(Predominant_simple) comprised five levels: Primary vegetation (n = 10,920),
Secondary (n = 8,603), Plantation forest (n = 7,122), Cropland (n = 4,577), and
Pasture (n = 3,431). Primary vegetation was used as the reference level throughout.

We simplified the original 11-category biome classification into four broader
categories (Biome4): Tropical Forest (Tropical & Subtropical Moist Broadleaf,
Dry Broadleaf, and Coniferous Forests; reference level), Tropical Open (Tropical &
Subtropical Grasslands/Savannas/Shrublands, Flooded Grasslands, Deserts &
Xeric Shrublands, Mangroves), Temperate Forest (Temperate Broadleaf & Mixed Forests,
Temperate Conifer Forests), and Temperate Open (Temperate Grasslands/Savannas/
Shrublands, Mediterranean Forests/Woodlands/Scrub, Montane Grasslands, Boreal
Forests/Taiga).

Body mass was log-transformed and standardised (z_logMass) prior to analysis.
Trophic niche categories from AVONET (Aquatic predator, Frugivore, Granivore,
Herbivore terrestrial, Invertivore, Nectarivore, Omnivore) were used as a fixed
factor, with Aquatic predator as the reference level.

For Analysis 3, we used a phylogenetic tree (BigBirdTree BBtree2; Newick format,
9,072 tips) employing Jetz-era taxonomy. Species were matched to tree tips in three
stages: (1) direct matching via a dedicated jetz_sp column (1,367 species); (2)
fallback matching via Best_guess_binomial with underscores (15 additional species);
and (3) synonym lookup for cases of genus reclassification between BirdLife and
BirdTree taxonomies (15 additional species; full synonym table available as
results/A3_synonym_table.csv). Three species pairs where multiple BirdLife names
mapped to a single Jetz species (Anthus cinnamomeus/richardi, Camaroptera
brachyura/brevicaudata, Zosterops kirki/maderaspatanus) were excluded to avoid
duplicate entries. This yielded 1,391 matched species (81.7% of the 1,703-species
dataset); the remaining 306 unmatched species had valid BirdTree names absent from
the BBtree2 tree. No species were grafted onto congeners. After additionally
excluding species lacking colour data, the phylogenetic models were fitted to
n = 1,254 species.

Statistical analyses

All models were fitted in a Bayesian framework using brms (version >= 2.18)
interfacing with CmdStan. We ran three complementary analyses.

Analysis 1: Community-level plumage colour ~ land-use type

We modelled how plumage colour varied across land-use types at the level of
individual occurrence records, fitting separate models for each of the four colour
responses. The preferred (reduced) model was:

    colour ~ Predominant_simple + z_logMass + Biome4 + Trophic.Niche
             + Predominant_simple:z_logMass
             + Predominant_simple:Biome4
             + Predominant_simple:Trophic.Niche
             + (1 | SSBS)

where SSBS denotes site nested within study nested within block (random intercept).
We also fitted a minimal model that omitted the Predominant_simple:Biome4 interaction
term and compared the two models using leave-one-out cross-validation (LOO-CV) via
the loo package. The reduced model was strongly preferred for all four responses
(elpd_diff relative to the minimal model: meancolcooney -122.2 ± 14.7 SE;
malecolcooney -126.2 ± 14.9; dichrodiff -89.9 ± 13.6; dichrocooney -67.6 ± 12.9),
and all subsequent results are from the reduced model.

Family and priors: meancolcooney, dichrocooney, and malecolcooney were modelled with
a lognormal family (priors: Normal(0, 2) on the intercept; Normal(0, 0.5) on all
fixed effects; Exponential(2) on the residual SD and sigma). dichrodiff, which can
take negative values, was modelled with a Gaussian family (priors: Normal(0, 50) on
the intercept; Normal(0, 20) on fixed effects; Exponential(2) on SD and sigma). All
models used 4 chains, 3,000 iterations per chain (1,500 warmup), adapt_delta = 0.90,
and within-chain parallelisation. The intercept in these models represents the
expected colour for a species of mean body mass, Aquatic predator trophic niche, in
Primary vegetation within a Tropical Forest biome.

All eight models converged satisfactorily (maximum Rhat ≤ 1.007; minimum bulk ESS
≥ 535; zero divergent transitions).

Analysis 2: Relative abundance ~ plumage colour × land-use type

We examined whether more colourful species had disproportionately higher or lower
relative abundance in particular land-use types. To remove variation in site-level
sampling intensity, abundance was expressed as each species' proportion of the total
recorded abundance at its site: for each record, we divided the effort-corrected
measurement by the sum of all effort-corrected measurements within that SSBS level.
Each colour predictor was standardised (z-scored) prior to modelling. For each of
the four colour variables separately, we fitted:

    relative_abundance ~ z_colour * Predominant_simple + (1 | SSBS)

using a lognormal family (priors: Normal(0, 2) on intercept; Normal(0, 0.5) on
fixed effects; Exponential(2) on SD and sigma). SSBS was retained as a random
intercept to account for residual non-independence among records from the same site
after the site-level normalisation. The main z_colour effect represents the change
in log-relative-abundance per 1 SD increase in colour within Primary vegetation;
interaction terms represent how this slope changes in each modified land use relative
to Primary. Models used 4 chains, 3,000 iterations (1,500 warmup), adapt_delta = 0.90,
and within-chain threading. Three of four models converged cleanly (Rhat ≤ 1.008;
ESS > 500; zero divergences); z_dichrodiff was marginal (max Rhat = 1.014, zero
divergences) and is interpreted with additional caution.

Analysis 3: Phylogenetic regression — plumage colour ~ land-use association

To test whether evolutionary lineages with stronger affinity for particular land-use
types differ systematically in colouration, we computed species-level land-use
proportions: for each species, the fraction of its records falling within each
Predominant_simple category. These within-species proportions remove the confound
with species-level prevalence and sum to 1 per species.

We fitted Bayesian phylogenetic regression models via brms using a no-intercept
parameterisation:

    colour ~ 0 + prop_Cropland + prop_Pasture + prop_Plantation_forest
                + prop_Primary_vegetation + prop_Secondary
                + (1 | gr(phylo, cov = A))

where A is the phylogenetic covariance matrix derived from ape::vcv.phylo() in
correlation form, and the gr() term captures residual covariance among species due
to shared ancestry. Because the five proportions sum to 1 per species, the
no-intercept specification is necessary to avoid perfect collinearity; each
coefficient is directly interpretable as the expected colour for a hypothetical
species recorded exclusively in that land-use type. Lognormal and Gaussian families
were used as in Analysis 1. Priors were broadened to reflect species-level rather
than record-level data: Normal(0, 5) on fixed effects for lognormal models;
Normal(0, 100) for the Gaussian (dichrodiff) model; Exponential(2) on SD and sigma
throughout. Models used 4 chains, 4,000 iterations (2,000 warmup), adapt_delta = 0.95,
max_treedepth = 12, and 4 threads per chain. All four models converged (zero
divergences; maximum Rhat ≤ 1.01; adequate ESS). The dichrodiff model produced
low E-BFMI values (< 0.3 on all chains), indicating potential difficulties sampling
the posterior energy distribution; those estimates are therefore interpreted with
additional caution.

Effect credibility is assessed throughout by whether the 95% posterior credible
interval (CI) excludes zero (for Gaussian responses) or one (in the untransformed
metric where appropriate). Bayesian R² values are reported as the posterior median
and 95% CI.


================================================================================
RESULTS
================================================================================

Analysis 1: Community-level plumage colour across land-use types

No land-use category showed a credible main effect on any of the four colour
responses, indicating that land-use type alone does not directly and uniformly shift
community-level plumage colour. Instead, the relevant effects operated through
interactions with biome, trophic niche, and body mass, as described below for each
response.

Body mass effects

Larger-bodied species were consistently less colourful: the standardised body-mass
effect was credibly negative for all four responses (meancolcooney: -0.115 [95% CI:
-0.122, -0.108]; malecolcooney: -0.125; dichrocooney: -0.023 [-0.027, -0.019];
dichrodiff: -4.19 [-4.78, -3.61]). The mass–colour relationship varied across land
uses: in both Pasture and Cropland, the negative association was credibly weakened
for meancolcooney (Pasture: +0.052; Cropland: +0.022), malecolcooney (Pasture:
+0.064; Cropland: +0.032), and both dichromatism metrics (dichrocooney Pasture:
+0.030; Cropland: +0.024; dichrodiff Pasture: +4.4; Cropland: +2.6). In contrast,
Plantation forest credibly strengthened the mass–dichromatism relationship
(dichrocooney: -0.015; dichrodiff: -1.2), meaning larger species in plantations
retained larger sex differences in colour than expected.

Biome and biome × land-use interactions

Biome was a strong predictor of community colour and dichromatism. For overall and
male colourfulness, all non-tropical-forest biomes had credibly less colourful
communities relative to Tropical Forest (Temperate Forest: meancolcooney -0.12,
malecolcooney -0.07; Temperate Open: -0.27, -0.23; Tropical Open: -0.20, -0.20).
The pattern for dichromatism was strikingly different: Temperate Forest communities
were credibly *more* sexually dichromatic than Tropical Forest communities
(dichrocooney: +0.12; dichrodiff: +11.5 LociUVS units), revealing a dissociation
between overall colour and sexual colour differences across biomes.

These biome effects were modified by land use in consistent ways. In Temperate Forest
biomes, Cropland and Plantation forest communities were credibly less colourful than
predicted by the biome main effect alone (meancolcooney Cropland: -0.09; Plantation:
-0.11; malecolcooney Cropland: -0.14; Plantation: -0.14), and the elevated baseline
dichromatism of temperate forest communities was credibly eroded by Cropland, Pasture,
and Plantation forest conversion (dichrocooney Cropland: -0.10; Pasture: -0.08;
Plantation: -0.07; dichrodiff Cropland: -11.2; Pasture: -10.1; Plantation: -12.0).
Secondary vegetation in Temperate Forest was the only modified land use that did not
credibly reduce the dichromatism signal (dichrocooney: -0.001; dichrodiff: -2.3,
borderline), suggesting that regenerating vegetation retains a community composition
more similar to primary temperate forest. Conversely, in Temperate Open biomes,
Secondary and Pasture communities were credibly more colourful than expected
(meancolcooney Secondary: +0.20; Pasture: +0.12; malecolcooney Secondary: +0.19).
In Tropical Open biomes, Plantation forest and Cropland communities were credibly
more colourful (meancolcooney Plantation: +0.17; Cropland: +0.08; malecolcooney
Plantation: +0.17; Cropland: +0.10), while Cropland credibly increased dichromatism
(dichrocooney: +0.04; dichrodiff Cropland: +7.6; Pasture: +3.2).

Trophic niche effects

Frugivores were the most colourful guild across all land uses (meancolcooney: +0.20,
credible; malecolcooney: +0.16, credible). For dichromatism, several guilds were
credibly less dichromatic than the Aquatic predator reference in Primary vegetation:
Frugivores (dichrocooney: -0.10), Invertivores (dichrocooney: -0.22;
dichrodiff: -20.1), and Omnivores (dichrocooney: -0.13; dichrodiff: -9.1). Nectarivores
showed the largest baseline sex difference (dichrodiff: +33.1, credible).

Trophic × land-use interactions revealed two particularly prominent patterns.
First, Plantation forest was credibly unfavourable for frugivore colourfulness: the
Plantation × Frugivore interaction was -0.35 (meancolcooney) and -0.36 (malecolcooney),
the strongest trophic interaction in the dataset. Second, Pasture communities showed
elevated male-biased colour differences in Frugivores (dichrodiff: +20.3, credible)
but eroded sex differences in Nectarivores (dichrodiff Pasture × Nectarivore: -19.7;
Secondary × Nectarivore: -17.4; both credible), demonstrating that the two
dichromatism metrics capture complementary aspects of community restructuring.

Model fit: Bayesian R² values were 16.2% (meancolcooney), 14.6% (malecolcooney),
10.4% (dichrocooney), and 14.1% (dichrodiff).

Analysis 2: Plumage colour as a predictor of relative abundance across land-use types

Bayesian R² was ~50.8% for all four models (meancolcooney: 50.8% [49.2–52.4%];
dichrocooney: 50.8% [49.2–52.4%]; malecolcooney: 50.8% [49.2–52.4%]; dichrodiff:
50.8% [49.3–52.3%]). This represents a substantial increase over models fitted to
raw abundance, reflecting that the site-level normalisation removes a large component
of between-site variation in overall recording effort.

Land-use main effects on relative abundance (estimated at mean colour, z = 0) were
credibly positive for Cropland (+0.45), Pasture (+0.62–0.63), and Secondary (+0.22)
across all four models. Plantation forest was near zero and not credible (~0.00),
in contrast to the large positive effect seen with raw abundance, indicating that
plantation forests do not differ from primary vegetation in overall community
composition once site-level sampling effort is accounted for.

None of the four main colour effects in Primary vegetation were credible
(z_meancolcooney: -0.01 [-0.02, 0.01]; z_dichrocooney: -0.00 [-0.02, 0.01];
z_malecolcooney: -0.01 [-0.02, 0.00]; z_dichrodiff: -0.01 [-0.03, 0.00]).
Unlike the raw-abundance analysis, there was no credible negative relationship
between dichromatism and relative abundance in primary vegetation.

The clearest colour × land-use interactions involved Pasture and Plantation forest.
The z_dichrodiff × Pasture interaction (+0.10 [+0.06, +0.14]) was the single
largest interaction in the analysis: species with greater male-biased colour
differences had higher relative abundance in pastures. The z_malecolcooney × Pasture
(+0.07 [+0.04, +0.11]) and z_dichrocooney × Pasture (+0.06 [+0.02, +0.09])
interactions were also credible. In contrast, Plantation forest credibly reduced
the relative abundance of more colourful species: z_meancolcooney × Plantation
(-0.04 [-0.06, -0.01]) and z_malecolcooney × Plantation (-0.03 [-0.05, -0.01])
were both credibly negative, a pattern not detected in the raw-abundance analysis.

For dichromatism, Cropland and Secondary vegetation also showed credible positive
interactions: z_dichrocooney × Cropland (+0.03 [+0.01, +0.06]), z_dichrocooney ×
Secondary (+0.03 [+0.01, +0.06]), and z_dichrodiff × Secondary (+0.04 [+0.01,
+0.06]). The meancolcooney and malecolcooney × Cropland and × Secondary interactions
were not credible or only borderline.

Analysis 3: Phylogenetic signal and land-use association

Plumage colour was strongly phylogenetically conserved: R² was 88.6% (95% CI:
84.6–91.8%) for meancolcooney and 86.2% (81.8–89.9%) for malecolcooney, with
phylogenetic standard deviations greatly exceeding residual standard deviations
(sd(phylo) = 0.52 vs. sigma = 0.14 for meancolcooney; 0.56 vs. 0.17 for
malecolcooney). Sexual dichromatism was less conserved: R² was 46.6% (37.1–57.1%)
for dichrocooney and 65.6% (57.9–72.9%) for dichrodiff.

Because phylogenetic variance dominated the colour responses, the land-use proportion
coefficients had wide, largely overlapping credible intervals. Nevertheless, a
consistent pattern emerged for both colourfulness metrics: species evolutionarily
associated with Pasture habitats had the lowest expected log-colour values
(meancolcooney: 4.42 [4.07, 4.79]; malecolcooney: 4.44 [4.05, 4.83]), while
Cropland-associated lineages were consistently most colourful (meancolcooney: 4.67
[4.31, 5.03]; malecolcooney: 4.68 [4.29, 5.07]). The Pasture–Primary vegetation
difference (~-0.15 on the log scale) was the most prominent pattern across both
colourfulness models. No land-use proportion credibly predicted dichromatism in
either dichrocooney or dichrodiff; all five land-use coefficients had heavily
overlapping CIs spanning zero (dichrodiff CIs spanning approximately ±25 units).

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Synthesis

Taken together, the three analyses reveal a complex but coherent picture. Land-use
type does not uniformly shift community-level colour: its effects are mediated by
biome, trophic ecology, and body mass (Analysis 1). The clearest community-level
signals are biome-dependent: land-use conversion in Temperate Forest erodes the
elevated baseline dichromatism of those communities, while conversion in Tropical
Open biomes tends to elevate colourfulness. Frugivore communities are strongly
reduced in colourfulness in Plantation forests, the strongest trophic interaction
identified. At the relative-abundance level (Analysis 2), species with greater sexual colour
differences are disproportionately more abundant in Pasture, Cropland, and Secondary
vegetation relative to Primary. Notably, Plantation forests credibly reduce the
relative abundance of more colourful species (overall and male colourfulness), while
showing no positive effect for dichromatism — a pattern absent from the raw-abundance
analysis and suggesting active filtering against colourful species in plantations.
The phylogenetic analysis (Analysis 3) shows that pasture-associated lineages are
inherently less colourful after accounting for shared ancestry, while no land-use
association predicts dichromatism at the evolutionary level. Together these results
indicate that the elevated relative abundance of sexually dichromatic species in
open and degraded habitats reflects ecological sorting and community reassembly
rather than evolutionary predisposition, and that plantations impose a distinct
filter that disfavours colourful lineages.
