METHODS AND RESULTS
Draft: avian plumage colour and land-use change
================================================================================
Four analyses (community colour, colour-abundance, colour-abundance change, and a phylogenetic test). Each is run twice: once with Cooney UVS colour (the A analyses, primary) and once with the independent Dale plumage scores (the B analyses, a colour-source robustness check), reported in separate sections. Model codes in parentheses map to scripts and ANALYSIS_NOTES.md.

STATUS, 2026-08-13. All four Cooney analyses, both sensitivity checks, and the Cooney figures are complete and written up below. In the Dale replication, B1b and B1a are complete and written up, B2c is fitting, and B2d and B3 follow. Every number previously reported in this draft came from a dataset built by a faulty filter and was removed rather than carried forward.

METHODS
================================================================================

Data

We combined the PREDICTS database (site-level bird survey records from studies spanning multiple land-use types worldwide) with Cooney et al.'s spectrophotometric plumage measurements (ultraviolet-sensitive [UVS] visual model, LociUVS scores) and AVONET traits (body mass, trophic niche). The merged dataset comprised 216,834 records from 1,703 passerine species. Our primary (A) analyses used the Cooney UVS model. We derived four species-level colour variables: mean colourfulness (mean of male and female LociUVS), male conspicuousness (male LociUVS), the sexual dichromatism ratio (male over female LociUVS), and the sexual dichromatism difference (male minus female LociUVS, which can be negative). We then repeated every analysis with the independent Dale et al. (2015) plumage scores in place of the UVS metrics, as a colour-source robustness check reported separately (the B analyses, see Dale-colour replication).

We collapsed the WWF biome field into three categories (Biome4): Tropical Forest (moist and dry broadleaf and coniferous forests, the reference), Tropical Open (tropical grasslands, savannas, shrublands, and mangroves), and Temperate Forest (temperate broadleaf, mixed and conifer forests, and Mediterranean forests, woodlands and scrub). A fourth category, Temperate Open (temperate and montane grasslands, tundra), was excluded. It contributed 115 primary-vegetation records against 36, 42, 0 and 35 records in the four modified land uses, so its biome by land-use interaction was not estimable and no collapse of those cells left a defensible contrast. Excluding it cost 0.9% of records. Land use (five levels) was Primary vegetation (reference), Secondary, Plantation forest, Cropland, and Pasture. Body mass was log-transformed and standardised (z_logMass). Trophic niche (AVONET) entered as a fixed factor (Aquatic predator reference).

Site-level analyses used records with non-zero abundance, in the retained biomes, from publications containing primary vegetation plus at least one other land use (n = 25,864 records, 1,420 species, "present.csv"). The biome exclusion preceded the primary-vegetation filter, so each publication was judged on the records that enter the models. Land-use representation was Primary vegetation 10,805, Plantation forest 6,482, Secondary 5,733, Cropland 2,225, and Pasture 619. All fifteen biome by land-use cells were estimable, the thinnest being Tropical Open by Pasture at 96 records from 34 species. Pasture is nonetheless sparse, because one contributing publication holding 1,387 pasture records contained no primary vegetation and was therefore excluded, and we interpret pasture effects with corresponding caution.

All models used the canonical PREDICTS nested random structure: study, block within study, and site within block (SS, SSB, SSBS, 40 studies / 203 blocks / 2,335 sites), which is fully nested because these identifiers are hierarchically coded. Analyses 2 to 4 additionally included a phylogenetic random effect from the BigBirdTree tree (BBtree2, 9,072 tips, Jetz taxonomy) as a Brownian-motion covariance matrix A (ape::vcv.phylo, correlation form). Species were matched to tips in three stages (jetz_sp, then Best_guess_binomial, then a curated genus-synonym table), with no grafting, and tips claimed by more than one species were dropped. Matching retained 1,157 species (19,785 records) for Analysis 2, 1,154 species (50,534 records) for Analysis 3, and 1,391 species (1,254 with Cooney colour data) at the species level.

Statistical framework

Models were fitted in brms/CmdStan. Colourfulness and dichromatism-ratio responses, and relative abundance, used a lognormal family. The dichromatism difference and the paired abundance difference used a Gaussian family. Effects are termed credible when the 95% posterior credible interval (CI) excludes zero. Bayesian R2 is reported as posterior median [95% CI].

Analysis 1: Community plumage colour across biomes and land uses (A1b, A1a)

For each of the four colour responses (community-weighted over occurrence records, n = 24,614 with non-missing colour), we fitted two nested models that share the same data, priors, sampler, and random structure. The primary model (A1b) was

    colour ~ Biome4 * Predominant_simple + Trophic.Niche + z_logMass
             + (1 | SS) + (1 | SSB) + (1 | SSBS)

We ran A1b in two variants, a base model with biome by land use only and a covariate model that adds trophic niche and body mass as main effects, and we report the covariate model. The extension (A1a) adds two interaction families to the A1b covariate model, land use by trophic niche and land use by body mass:

    colour ~ Biome4 * Predominant_simple + Trophic.Niche + z_logMass
             + Predominant_simple:z_logMass + Predominant_simple:Trophic.Niche
             + (1 | SS) + (1 | SSB) + (1 | SSBS)

A1a is therefore a nested superset of A1b, and we compared the two by leave-one-out cross-validation (LOO) to test whether the guild-specific and size-specific interactions earn their place. Priors were Normal(0,2) on the intercept, Normal(0,0.5) on fixed effects, and Exponential(2) on the standard deviations and sigma for the lognormal responses, and Normal(0,50), Normal(0,20), Exponential(2) for the Gaussian response. We ran 4 chains for 3,000 iterations (1,500 warmup) at adapt_delta = 0.90. No phylogenetic term was included. Because colour is a species-level constant with no within-species variance, a per-species phylogenetic intercept is not identifiable at the record level, where it absorbs all between-species variance and collapses the residual and site terms. The phylogenetic question is addressed at the species level in Analysis 4. All twelve models converged with no divergent transitions. For A1b the maximum Rhat was 1.008 and the minimum bulk effective sample size (ESS) was 651. For A1a the maximum Rhat was 1.011 and the minimum bulk ESS was 539.

Analysis 2: Colour as a predictor of relative abundance (A2c)

We tested whether colour predicts a species' share of its community, and whether that share depends on biome or land use. Relative abundance was each species' effort-corrected count divided by its site (SSBS) total, and each colour metric was standardised. For male conspicuousness and the dichromatism difference we fitted (lognormal)

    relative_abundance ~ z_colour * Biome4 + z_colour * Predominant_simple
                         + Biome4 * Predominant_simple
                         + (1|SS) + (1|SSB) + (1|SSBS) + (1 | gr(phylo, cov = A))

that is, all three two-way interactions but no three-way term. Priors were as in Analysis 1 (lognormal), with 4 chains by 2,000 iterations (1,000 warmup) at adapt_delta = 0.90. Because colour, trophic niche and body mass are themselves phylogenetically conserved, the colour main effect is interpreted as an effect beyond phylogeny. As a sensitivity check we also fitted the full three-way interaction (model A2e) and compared it to the two-way model by LOO. Both models converged with no divergent transitions, a maximum Rhat of 1.006, and a minimum bulk ESS of 846.

Analysis 3: Colour and abundance change following land-use conversion (A2d)

To include absences and anchor each species to its own baseline, we computed, per study, each species' mean relative abundance across primary-vegetation sites, and subtracted it from that species' relative abundance at each modified site (the paired difference). Exact-zero differences, meaning species absent from both, were dropped as uninformative. This yielded 66,225 non-zero differences, of which 54,038 were negative. Primary vegetation is thus absorbed into the response, leaving four land-use levels. For the same two colour metrics we fitted the same two-way structure as Analysis 2 with a Gaussian family (n = 50,534). Priors were Normal(0,0.5), Normal(0,0.2), and Exponential(5), and we ran 4 chains for 3,500 iterations (1,500 warmup) at adapt_delta = 0.95, max_treedepth = 12. The three-way interaction was again fitted as a sensitivity check (model A2f) and compared by LOO. Both models converged with no divergent transitions, a maximum Rhat of 1.007, and a minimum bulk ESS of 587. The elevated intercept Rhat seen in the previous fit did not recur.

Analysis 4: Phylogenetic association of colour with land use (A3)

At the species level, we regressed each colour response on the within-species proportion of records in each land use (five compositional proportions summing to 1), using a no-intercept parameterisation so each coefficient is the expected colour for a species recorded exclusively in that land use:

    colour ~ 0 + prop_Cropland + prop_Pasture + prop_Plantation_forest
                + prop_Primary_vegetation + prop_Secondary + (1 | gr(phylo, cov = A))

These proportions were computed from the full raw dataset rather than the filtered analytical dataset, because a species' habitat association is a property of the species and is best estimated from all of its records. Priors were broadened for species-level data: Normal(0,5) on fixed effects for the lognormal responses, Normal(0,50) for the Gaussian response, Exponential(2) on the standard deviations and sigma for the lognormal responses, and Exponential(0.05) for the Gaussian response. We ran 4 chains for 4,000 iterations (2,000 warmup) at adapt_delta = 0.95, max_treedepth = 12, for n = 1,254 species. All four models converged on the usual diagnostics, with no divergent transitions, a maximum Rhat of 1.002, and a minimum bulk ESS of 863. All four nonetheless returned an energy Bayesian fraction of missing information (E-BFMI) below 0.3, on all four chains for mean colourfulness, male conspicuousness and the dichromatism difference, and on one chain for the dichromatism ratio. A low E-BFMI indicates that the sampler explored the energy distribution poorly, which is common in hierarchical models with a heavy-tailed posterior and is not detected by Rhat or effective sample size. We therefore treat the A3 posteriors as adequate for central estimates but unreliable in the tails, and we return to this when interpreting the land-use contrasts. Because the compositional coefficients are strongly correlated in the posterior, we compare land uses by contrasting posterior draws rather than by inspecting whether marginal credible intervals overlap, and we report those contrasts with the posterior probability that the difference is positive.

Dale-colour replication (B analyses)

To test whether the findings depend on the colour-scoring system, we repeated all four analyses with Dale et al.'s plumage scores in place of the Cooney UVS metrics, holding the data, priors, sampler, random structure, and metric families identical. These are the B analyses: B1b and B1a replicate A1b and A1a, B2c replicates A2c, B2d replicates A2d, and B3 replicates A3. From each colour system we derived the same four responses (mean colourfulness, male conspicuousness, the dichromatism ratio, and the dichromatism difference), with the same lognormal and Gaussian families. Dale scores cover more species than the UVS metrics (1,679 against 1,530 with a male score), so the B runs retained more records (n = 25,692 at the community level). The B outputs are kept separate from the A analyses throughout (scripts/dale, results/dale, figures/pub/dale).

RESULTS
================================================================================

Analysis 1: Community plumage colour (A1b, A1a)

Land-use conversion dulls bird communities, modestly and in every direction. All four modified land uses held credibly less colourful communities than primary vegetation, for both mean colourfulness (Cropland -0.078, Pasture -0.089, Plantation forest -0.047, Secondary -0.033 on the log scale) and male conspicuousness (-0.081, -0.082, -0.050, -0.035). The effect is small against the range of the response, and it is remarkably even across land uses that differ greatly in how much they alter vegetation structure. Neither dichromatism measure showed a comparable pattern. The dichromatism ratio was flat across all four land uses, and only cropland moved the dichromatism difference (-2.6 LociUVS units).

Biome mattered more than land use, and colour and dichromatism came apart. Temperate Forest communities were duller than tropical forest ones (mean -0.183, male -0.136) yet credibly more sexually dichromatic (ratio +0.094, difference +7.1). Tropical Open communities differed from tropical forest on no metric once trophic niche and body mass were included. This dissociation between overall colourfulness and the size of the sex difference is the clearest structural result in the analysis, and it runs in opposite directions within the same communities.

Body mass was the strongest single predictor. Larger birds were less colourful and less dichromatic on every metric (mean -0.123, male -0.136, ratio -0.032, difference -5.0), and each estimate was tightly bounded. Among guilds, invertivores stood out as less colourful (male -0.179) and markedly less dichromatic (ratio -0.197, difference -18.7), while nectarivores carried by far the largest sex differences (difference +22.8) without being credibly more colourful. Frugivores and omnivores were less dichromatic than the reference guild on the ratio scale.

Biome by land-use interactions were few and modest. Conversion in tropical open habitats raised dichromatism above what the main effects predict, in cropland (ratio +0.051, difference +8.3) and plantation forest (ratio +0.031, difference +3.1). Pasture communities were more colourful than expected in both temperate forest (+0.075) and tropical open (+0.086) biomes. Adding trophic niche and body mass roughly doubled explanatory power over the biome by land-use base model, from 9.0% to 16.3% for mean colour, 7.0% to 15.1% for male colour, 7.1% to 14.2% for the ratio, and 7.6% to 18.1% for the difference.

Guild-specific and size-specific responses to land use earn their place. LOO favoured A1a over A1b for all four responses, by 350 (SE 37), 220 (SE 28), 356 (SE 36), and 248 (SE 32) units of expected log predictive density, each between 8 and 10 standard errors. Bayesian R2 rose to 19.0% (mean colour), 17.9% (male), 15.9% (ratio), and 19.9% (difference).

Frugivores respond to land use in opposite directions depending on the habitat. In plantation forest they were credibly duller (mean -0.270, male -0.286), the single strongest guild effect in the earlier analyses and one that survives here. In the two open land uses they were the reverse, and more strongly so: cropland +0.362 and pasture +0.447 for mean colour, pasture +0.465 for male colour, and pasture +27.7 for the dichromatism difference. Plantations and open agriculture therefore do not act on frugivores in the same way, and treating "modified land" as one category would cancel these two effects against each other.

Nectarivores lose their sex difference wherever primary vegetation is replaced. Against a nectarivore baseline of +31.5 in primary vegetation, the dichromatism difference fell credibly in cropland (-31.8), pasture (-42.5), and secondary vegetation (-21.8). In cropland and pasture the interaction is as large as the baseline or larger, so the elevated nectarivore sex difference is not merely reduced there but erased. The one exception ran the other way: in plantation forest nectarivores were credibly more dichromatic on the ratio scale (+0.197). Granivores in secondary vegetation (+13.6) and omnivores in cropland (+20.6) also gained sex difference.

The body-size effect strengthens under conversion rather than relaxing. Larger birds became relatively duller still in cropland (mean -0.039, male -0.046) and secondary vegetation (-0.028, -0.036), and relatively less dichromatic in cropland, plantation forest, and secondary vegetation alike (ratio -0.015 to -0.019, difference -1.5 to -2.1). Only male conspicuousness in pasture ran the other way (+0.036). Large-bodied species are therefore doubly disadvantaged in converted habitat, being both duller at baseline and duller again relative to that baseline.

Analysis 2: Colour as a predictor of relative abundance (A2c)

Colour does not predict how abundant a species is within its community. Not one colour by land-use interaction was credible, for either male conspicuousness or the dichromatism difference, and neither colour main effect was credible (male +0.011, difference -0.012). These are well-estimated zeros rather than an absence of power: bulk ESS was at least 846 for every parameter and the intervals are narrow, for example male colour by pasture at -0.017 [-0.082, +0.048].

One colour effect survived, and it is biome-level rather than land-use-level. More colourful males were credibly less relatively abundant in tropical open communities (-0.094 [-0.166, -0.017]). This term survives precisely because biome varies within a species, so it is not absorbed by the per-species phylogenetic effect in the way that a species-constant colour value is.

What structures relative abundance is lineage and study, not colour. The model explained 53.2% of variance, and that variance sat almost entirely in the phylogenetic effect (sd 0.97) and the study effect (sd 0.95), well above the residual (sd 0.65) and far above site (0.25) and block (0.20). Among land uses only pasture stood out, with higher per-species relative abundance (+0.351), which is what an impoverished and more dominated community looks like. Conversion in temperate forest raised relative abundance in plantation forest (+0.206) and secondary vegetation (+0.234) and lowered it in pasture (-0.365).

Analysis 3: Colour and abundance change following land-use conversion (A2d)

Colour barely moves how much abundance a species gains or loses when its habitat is converted. No colour main effect was credible, for either metric, and only one colour by land-use interaction reached credibility: male conspicuousness in plantation forest, at +0.0018 [+0.0004, +0.0032]. On a response whose residual standard deviation is 0.050 and whose values run to roughly plus or minus 0.06, an effect of 0.002 is negligible. We regard it as credible but not meaningful.

Three colour by biome interactions were credible and are also small. More conspicuous males lost slightly less abundance in temperate forest (+0.0100) and tropical open habitats (+0.0086), and species with larger sex differences lost slightly less in tropical open habitats (+0.0106). These are the largest colour effects anywhere in the analysis and they still amount to about a fifth of the residual standard deviation.

What does structure abundance change is lineage. The model explained 23.1% of variance, almost all of it carried by the phylogenetic random effect (sd 0.072), which exceeded the residual (sd 0.050) and dwarfed study (sd 0.015), block (sd 0.0011), and site (sd 0.0002). Related species therefore gain and lose abundance together under land-use conversion, and they do so for reasons that colour does not capture. Among land uses, only cropland showed a credible mean loss relative to the other modified types (-0.0039), and that loss was steeper still in tropical open habitats (-0.0055).

Read alongside Analysis 2, the two abundance results agree. Colour does not predict a species' standing share of its community, and it does not predict how that share changes when the habitat is converted. Note the sign contrast on tropical open habitats, where more conspicuous males held a lower standing share (-0.094 in Analysis 2) yet lost marginally less against their own baseline (+0.0086 here). These are different questions, one about level and one about change, so the two are not in conflict.

Analysis 4: Phylogenetic association of colour with land use (A3)

Plumage colour is strongly phylogenetically conserved. Phylogeny alone explained 88.6% of variance in mean colourfulness and 86.3% in male conspicuousness, with the phylogenetic standard deviation far exceeding the residual (0.515 against 0.143, and 0.560 against 0.173). The two dichromatism measures were less conserved, at 65.7% for the difference and 46.8% for the ratio. The magnitude of the sex difference is therefore more evolutionarily labile than colourfulness itself, and the ratio is the most labile trait we measured.

Species associated with pasture are credibly duller than species associated with primary vegetation. On the expected-colour scale the ordering ran Cropland 4.67, Secondary 4.61, Plantation forest 4.59, Primary vegetation 4.57, and Pasture 4.42 for mean colourfulness, with male conspicuousness in the same order. Read as marginal credible intervals these coefficients overlap almost entirely, each spanning roughly 0.7 units, and the pasture difference would appear unsupported. That reading is wrong. Because the five proportions sum to 1 per species, their coefficients are strongly correlated in the posterior, and the correct test is a contrast of the posterior draws. Contrasting pasture against primary vegetation gives -0.156 [-0.263, -0.045] for mean colourfulness and -0.147 [-0.273, -0.023] for male conspicuousness, with the posterior probability of a positive difference at 0.003 and 0.010 respectively.

No other land use differed credibly from primary vegetation, and neither dichromatism measure showed any land-use association at all. The pasture effect on colourfulness is therefore the single result of this analysis, and it is the only place in the study where a land-use association survives at the level of lineages rather than communities.

This result carries a caveat. Every A3 model returned a low E-BFMI, so the tails of these posteriors are not well characterised, and a contrast whose credibility rests on a 2.5% quantile is exactly the quantity such a warning bears on. The pasture contrast sits comfortably away from zero for mean colourfulness, at a posterior probability of 0.003, and we regard it as sound. The male-conspicuousness contrast is closer to the boundary at 0.010 and should be treated as suggestive until the sampling issue is resolved. Reparameterising the model, or raising adapt_delta and lengthening the warmup, would settle both.

Three-way sensitivity checks (A2e, A2f)

The three-way colour by biome by land-use interaction earns no place in either abundance model. For relative abundance (A2e) the three-way model was worse than the reported two-way model by 4.4 units of expected log predictive density (SE 3.2), and for the paired difference (A2f) by 1.3 units (SE 3.7). Neither difference reaches two standard errors, so the two models are statistically indistinguishable and parsimony selects the two-way form. We therefore report A2c and A2d throughout. Both checks used male conspicuousness.

Dale-colour replication (B analyses)

The community-colour architecture replicates under Dale scores, but the guild story does not. As with the Cooney metrics, LOO favoured the full-interaction model B1a over B1b for all four responses, by 97 to 158 units of expected log predictive density (SE 16 to 25), so guild-specific and size-specific land-use responses earn their place under either colour system. All twelve models converged with no divergent transitions, at a maximum Rhat of 1.012.

Body size behaves the same way under both colour systems. The mass by land-use interactions were credible in matching directions, positive in pasture (mean +0.012, male +0.018) and negative in plantation forest and secondary vegetation (-0.007 to -0.009 for the colour metrics). This independent corroboration matters, because these are the interactions whose sign changed when the primary-vegetation filter was corrected, and Dale scores reproduce the corrected pattern.

The trophic interactions do not replicate. Under Dale scores not one land use by trophic niche interaction was credible on any of the four metrics. The Cooney headline, frugivores duller in plantation forest, is absent: -0.070 [-0.185, +0.046] for mean colour and -0.075 [-0.212, +0.066] for male colour, against credible Cooney values of -0.270 and -0.286. The nectarivore results are likewise absent.

Dale scores also carry much less land-use-linked signal for colourfulness. Bayesian R2 for the covariate models was 8.6% for mean colour and 9.1% for male colour, roughly half the Cooney values of 16.3% and 15.1%. For the two dichromatism measures the position reverses, at 18.7% for the ratio and 19.4% for the difference against Cooney's 14.2% and 18.1%. The two scoring systems evidently capture overlapping but non-identical aspects of plumage, and guild-level colour signals are the ones most sensitive to that choice.
